Chapter 4

Methods

From raw Landsat scenes to the trend tests and the detrended correlations.

Workflow

  1. Select scenes

    One January or February Landsat Level-2 scene per year, path 143 row 47, cloud cover under 10%.

  2. Compute NDVI

    Convert stored values to reflectance and calculate NDVI from the red and near-infrared bands in QGIS.

  3. Build zones and take means

    Buffer the nine dissolved leases at 1, 3 and 5 km, clip each NDVI raster to the 5 km area, and take the mean NDVI of each zone with zonal statistics.

  4. Test the trend

    Run Mann–Kendall and Sen's slope on each zone series for 2005–2026, with autocorrelation checks.

  5. Compute anomalies and align years

    Convert NDVI, rainfall and temperature to departures from their 2005–2025 means and pair each NDVI year with the right climate and production year.

  6. Correlate, then remove the trend

    Correlate NDVI anomalies with each driver, then repeat after linear detrending and with first differences.

  7. Regress

    Fit least-squares models with year, rainfall and opencast production to see what each adds beyond time.

NDVI

Landsat Collection 2 Level-2 products store surface reflectance as scaled integers. Following the USGS scale factors, reflectance is ρ = 0.0000275 × DN − 0.2. NDVI is then

NDVI = ρNIR − ρRedρNIR + ρRed

using bands 3 and 4 for TM and ETM+, and bands 4 and 5 for OLI. Water is negative, bare soil, rock and coal are near zero, and dense green canopy is above about 0.5. Because the scenes are from the dry season, when many fields are fallow, zone means are low: between 0.12 and 0.21.

Anomalies

For each series xt, the anomaly is its departure from the 2005–2025 mean: At = xt − x̄. The same reference period is used for NDVI, rainfall and temperature, so a positive anomaly always means greener, wetter or warmer than usual. Reference means were 0.1572, 0.1638 and 0.1644 for the three NDVI zones, 1153.9 mm for annual rainfall, 988.2 mm for monsoon rainfall, 27.59 °C for annual temperature and 24.44 °C for January–February.

Lining up the years

A January or February scene reflects the monsoon that ended a few months earlier. The series were paired like this:

  • NDVI of year t with monsoon and annual rainfall of year t − 1 (the 2005 scene with the 2004 monsoon).
  • NDVI of year t with annual temperature of year t − 1, and January–February temperature of year t.
  • NDVI of year t with the financial year ending in March of year t (FY 2004–05 with the February 2005 scene).

That leaves 21 matched years, 2005 to 2025.

Trend tests

The Mann–Kendall test checks whether a series rises or falls steadily, without assuming a straight line or normal errors. It compares every pair of years:

S = Σi<j sgn(xj − xi),   Var(S) = n(n − 1)(2n + 5)18

S is standardised to Z, and a trend is significant at α = 0.05 when |Z| > 1.96. Kendall's τ = S / [n(n − 1)/2] gives its strength on a scale from −1 to +1. The rate of change is Sen's slope, the median of all pairwise slopes (xj − xi)/(j − i), which single extreme years cannot pull around.

The basic test assumes the yearly values are independent, but a trending series is usually autocorrelated. Two variants were run as checks: the Hamed–Rao variance correction and a pre-whitened test that removes lag-1 autocorrelation first. All tests used the pyMannKendall package.

Why the trend has to be removed before correlating

A child's height and the price of petrol both go up every year. Correlate them and you get a number close to 1, yet a birthday does not move fuel prices. Any two series that rise over the same years will look strongly linked, whether they are or not.

NDVI and opencast coal production both rose between 2005 and 2025, so a raw correlation between them mostly measures that shared rise. To test whether they are actually linked, the shared rise has to be taken out first:

  1. Fit a straight line through each series against the year

    For the overall NDVI anomaly the best-fit line is −5.264 + 0.00261 × year. For opencast production it is −40,480.6 + 20.31 × year.

  2. Subtract the line from the actual value in each year

    What is left, the residual, says how unusual that year was. NDVI in 2012 was 0.0265 below its line; opencast output in FY 2020–21 was 106 lakh tonnes below its line.

  3. Correlate the residuals

    If one series really drives the other, unusually high years in one should line up with unusual years in the other.

As a second check, the same correlations were run on first differences, the change from one year to the next (xt − xt−1), which also removes a steady trend. With 21 years, |r| needs to exceed about 0.43 for p < 0.05.

b, a = np.polyfit(year, values, 1)      # fit the straight line
residual = values - (a + b * year)      # what is left after removing it
